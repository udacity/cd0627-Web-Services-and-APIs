| Large language models are trained on massive public datasets, but they know nothing about your company's internal documentation, your product catalog, or yesterday's support tickets. If you ask a model a question about your proprietary data, it will either confidently hallucinate an answer or tell you it does not know. Retrieval-Augmented Generation—RAG—solves this by giving the model relevant context from your own data at query time, without retraining or fine-tuning the model itself. In this lesson, we are going to understand the core Naive RAG pipeline: how documents are ingested, how relevant context is retrieved, and how it is injected into the prompt to ground the model's response in your actual data. | |
| The RAG pipeline has two distinct phases: an offline ingestion phase that prepares your data, and an online query phase that retrieves and generates answers. The ingestion phase runs once—or on a schedule—to process your source documents. First, a document reader loads raw content from files like PDFs, Markdown, or HTML. Then a chunking step splits those documents into smaller segments. Chunking is critical because embedding models and LLMs have finite context windows—you cannot feed an entire 200-page manual into a single prompt. The standard approach is to split by token count using a `TokenTextSplitter`, with overlap between chunks to ensure that ideas split across chunk boundaries are not lost entirely. Each chunk is then passed through an embedding model to generate a vector, and the vector—along with the original text—is stored in a vector database. After ingestion, your data is searchable by meaning. | **Complex Visual:** Design a horizontal pipeline flowing left to right with four stages. | The online query phase is where the user interacts with the system. When a user asks a question, the system first converts that question into a vector using the same embedding model from the ingestion phase. It then searches the vector database for the chunks whose vectors are most similar to the question vector—this is the retrieval step. The top-K most relevant chunks are then injected into the LLM's prompt as context, alongside the user's original question. The model generates its response grounded in that retrieved context rather than relying solely on its training data. This is the "augmented" in Retrieval-Augmented Generation—you are augmenting the model's knowledge with real-time, domain-specific information. The result is factually grounded answers without the cost and complexity of fine-tuning or retraining the model. | **Complex Visual:** Design a vertical flow diagram with three steps. | The design decisions in the ingestion phase have a disproportionate impact on RAG quality. Chunk size is the most important: too small, and each chunk lacks enough context for the model to generate a coherent answer. Too large, and you waste the model's context window on irrelevant information, or exceed the embedding model's input limit entirely. The typical starting point is 300 to 500 tokens per chunk, with a 50-to-100-token overlap. The embedding model you choose determines the quality of your semantic matching—larger models capture more nuance but are slower and more expensive. And the retrieval parameters—top-K and similarity threshold—control the precision-recall trade-off: returning too many chunks dilutes quality; returning too few risks missing critical information. These are engineering decisions, not configuration defaults. Getting them right requires iterating against your specific data and your specific query patterns. | |
| In Spring AI 2.0, the RAG pattern is implemented through the advisor pattern. Instead of writing manual retrieval-and-injection logic, you attach a `QuestionAnswerAdvisor` to your `ChatClient`. The advisor intercepts the user's prompt, searches the configured `VectorStore`, retrieves the relevant chunks, injects them into the prompt as context, and passes the enriched prompt to the LLM—all transparently. Your application code remains a clean `ChatClient` call. You configure the advisor with the top-K count and similarity threshold, and it handles the retrieval pipeline internally. This separation of concerns keeps your business logic clean and makes the RAG behavior composable—you can chain it with other advisors, like a `MessageChatMemoryAdvisor` for conversation history, without modifying your core application flow.
